## Part 1: Hiring Manager First Impression

### What would an Auster hiring manager think after reading this bio?

"This person is thoughtful, put real effort into presenting himself, and is clearly serious about doing physical outdoor work — but I have no idea if he can lead a guided hike or talk to a guest about a grizzly track." The page reads as a **generic ranch/grounds hand**, not a **Yellowstone-adjacent resort host**. It proves the facilities half of the job reasonably well and is silent on the entire guided-recreation/interpretation half. A manager hiring for a hybrid role would likely see this as "strong maintenance candidate, unproven recreation candidate."

### What would immediately stand out positively

- **The page itself is the pitch.** Building a clean, custom, well-coded personal site for a groundskeeping/hospitality application is well above the bar — indirect but strong evidence of "personable" and "hard-working."
- **The "Where I Can Contribute" grid** (index.html:647-693) maps almost one-to-one onto Auster's Facilities & Property Operations pillar — groundskeeper, ranch support, landscaping, property maintenance, trail/outdoor ops.
- **Logistics are pre-answered**: exact start date, seasonal-or-long-term flexibility, relocation, housing openness (index.html:695-707).
- **Physical stamina proof is concrete, not asserted** — "8+ hour shifts," bike courier in Denver, all-weather work (index.html:752-798).
- **Tone**: humble, first-person, low-ego — "I respect that steady work," "willing to learn local systems, take direction well" (index.html:704, index.html:716).

### What would create doubts or concerns

- **Zero mention of guided hikes, wildlife viewing, fly-fishing, natural history, or Yellowstone interpretation** — a full third of the job description, entirely absent.
- **Facilities language is generic, not JD-specific.** No "snow removal," "pool/hot tub," or "preventative maintenance" — only "small fixes," "troubleshooting," and one glancing snow reference (index.html:784).
- **No regional specificity.** Nothing mentions Montana, Yellowstone, western heritage, or land stewardship in a place-specific way.
- **The brand mismatch.** Auster is a premium new resort, not a working cattle ranch. The site's utilitarian framing ("hauling," "chores," "cleanup") undersells the polish and guest-facing communication skill this employer needs.
- **The video is a black box.** "The Journey to Stewardship" (index.html:801-806) autoplays muted with no caption explaining what it is or why to watch it.

### What strengths are hidden but not obvious

- **Landscape architecture master's + environmental systems background** — reduced to "irrigation exposure"; its relevance to land stewardship/interpretation is invisible.
- **11+ years of UX/research/communication experience** — the exact skill behind "guest education" and "Yellowstone interpretation," never connected to it.
- **41 countries of travel** — used only to argue adaptability, never mined for recreation/interpretation credibility (e.g., firsthand exposure to guided experiences abroad).
- **Human-centered design mindset** — a real edge for "creating memorable guest experiences," currently invisible.

### Would I likely get an interview based solely on this page?

**Probably — for a screening conversation, not a confident yes.** The facilities/property/hospitality-support case is made well enough to earn a call. But because guided recreation and interpretation is roughly half the role and the page offers zero evidence there, a careful hiring manager is more likely to bring Dan in *to ask about it* than to assume it's covered. **This page should not go out to Auster unchanged.**

## Part 4: Risk Analysis

### Too corporate
- **Why:** Sentence-level tone is warm, but the page's *structure* isn't — the "Where I Can Contribute" grid is a literal capability matrix (index.html:651-691), and labels like "Field Readiness" (index.html:743) read as consulting/ops vocabulary.
- **Severity:** Low-Medium.
- **Fix:** Keep sentence-level warmth; soften structural labels ("Field Readiness," "Operations Support") toward plainer language.

### Too tech-focused
- **Why:** No mention of UX/design/tech anywhere in the visible text — but the linked LinkedIn profile (index.html:819) likely leads with the design career with none of the bio's framing.
- **Severity:** Low on-page; Medium in practice via the LinkedIn click.
- **Fix:** No change to bio text; align LinkedIn headline/About with the same pivot narrative before applying.

### Too academic
- **Why:** The landscape architecture degree is fully anonymized (index.html:712) — no explicit academic language currently exists.
- **Severity:** Very Low currently.
- **Fix:** When naming the degree (per Part 3), frame it in applied terms, not academic terms, so closing the credibility gap doesn't reopen this risk.

### Overqualified
- **Why:** The page's polish (custom nav, smooth-scroll tracking, restrained design) signals real design/dev skill even without being told. Combined with "10+ years professional experience" (index.html:630) and "I've spent much of my professional life solving problems..." (index.html:712), a reader can infer a substantial prior career, which tends to read as more suspicious unexplained than explained.
- **Severity:** Medium.
- **Fix:** Own the pivot in one confident sentence that preempts "why is this person applying here" rather than letting it be inferred.

### Underqualified
- **Why:** Facilities depth — past roles are undated/unquantified, no specific tools/tasks named. Guiding/safety — no Wilderness First Aid, CPR, or safety credential, which matters given explicit "guest safety" duties in bear/water-adjacent terrain.
- **Severity:** Medium on facilities depth; Medium-High on the guiding/safety axis.
- **Fix:** Quantify durations and name specific tools/tasks. Be upfront about the safety-cert gap paired with fast-learner evidence rather than staying silent.

### Detached from hands-on work
- **Why:** Several sections talk *about* the idea of hands-on work rather than from inside it — "I enjoy practical problem-solving" (index.html:644), the reflective tone of "Why Outdoor Work" (index.html:709-718), and the argumentation of "Travel as Field Reliability" (index.html:720-741) explaining why travel photos should count as work evidence.
- **Severity:** Medium — offset by the genuinely concrete skill-grid section.
- **Fix:** Shift toward plain, sensory, task-level sentences; trim reflective/meta passages.

### Uninterested in hospitality
- **Why:** Hospitality is named twice (index.html:682-691) but as two of seven equally-weighted job categories, not a personal inclination — no anecdote of enjoying hosting or organizing experiences for others.
- **Severity:** Medium.
- **Fix:** Add one authentic personal hospitality-adjacent detail (hosting friends, cooking for groups, organizing trips for others).

### Uninterested in guest interaction
- **Why:** Every "guest" mention pairs guests with maintaining their environment, never direct engagement — "back-of-house tasks" (index.html:683), "clean, welcoming outdoor spaces" (index.html:688). Not one sentence describes Dan talking with, teaching, or engaging a guest/stranger one-on-one, despite 41 countries of travel and an 11-year client/stakeholder-facing career almost certainly containing this evidence.
- **Severity:** High — the single highest-stakes risk in this audit, since guided hikes/fly-fishing/interpretation are hours of sustained interpersonal engagement.
- **Fix:** Add explicit, concrete evidence of enjoying direct interpersonal engagement. Highest-leverage fix in the entire audit.

**Priority order:** Uninterested in guest interaction (High) and the guiding/safety half of Underqualified (Medium-High) are most likely to cost an interview outright. Overqualified, Detached from hands-on work, and Uninterested in hospitality (Medium) are next. Too corporate, too tech-focused, and too academic are Low as the page stands today.

## Part 6: Website Rewrite Recommendations

### Hero Section
Headline should communicate both halves of the role (land care *and* people care), not just "Outdoor Operations." Should signal deliberate regional intent, not generic outdoor enthusiasm. Should not lead with "10+ years professional experience" unqualified (index.html:630). Tone should stay warm and drop ops-consultant vocabulary.

### Intro Paragraph
Should lead with concrete land-based experience (golf course, landscaping), not travel — currently the first proof point is the 41-countries line (index.html:643). Should state comfort with people, not just land, in the same breath. One brief, named nod to the design/landscape-architecture background. Should close on long-term intent.

### About Section *(currently "Why Outdoor Work," index.html:709-718)*
Frame as continuity, not a break — training was always oriented toward land and people; this role is the next chapter. Build around one concrete origin moment rather than general reflection. Name the landscape architecture degree tied to something applied. Include one honest line acknowledging guiding/interpretation as a growth area.

### Experience Section
Highlight: golf course grounds crew (named, dated, task-specific); landscaping/physical labor with durations; client-facing communication/research reframed as "explains complex things clearly"; personal recreation as lived practice; at least one direct people/guest-interaction moment; JD-mirrored task language (preventative maintenance, snow removal, pool/hot tub, equipment maintenance) wherever true. Every entry should pair a claim with a specific instance.

### Values Section *(new section)*
Ranked by strength of existing evidence:
1. Stewardship — backed by landscape architecture training + environmental interest + travel-taught respect for place.
2. Service mindset / hospitality — backed by existing warmth, strengthened by a real interaction story.
3. Adaptability — already well-proven (career pivot, 41 countries, comfort with change).
4. Willingness to learn — evidenced by a career spent moving into new domains.
5. Hard work / reliability — strongly evidenced (bike courier, all-weather work).
6. Honesty — best expressed by owning the guiding/interpretation growth area directly.

Deliberately not leading with "adventure/exploration" here — already the page's most dominant theme elsewhere; use Values to carry the true-but-under-evidenced traits instead.

## Part 8: Executive Summary

**Biggest Strength**
Reliability and physical work ethic, proven rather than asserted — the bike-courier detail is specific and checkable, backed by genuine adaptability evidence and unusually clear logistics.

**Biggest Weakness**
Zero evidence for the guided-recreation and guest-interpretation half of the role — not weak evidence, no evidence. Structural, not cosmetic.

**Most Overlooked Advantage**
Eleven years translating complex systems into language non-experts understand, backed by a landscape architecture degree — currently reduced to one anonymized clause. This is functionally the exact skill "Yellowstone interpretation" and "guest education" require.

**Fastest Improvement**
Name the golf course grounds crew role and the landscape architecture degree outright. Zero new experience required — just replacing vague phrasing with true, specific nouns.

**Final Recommendation**
Don't send this page to Auster as-is. Before applying, close the recreation/guiding gap with real material, surface the two hidden credentials, and add one genuine sentence about why this landscape specifically.

### Would I personally recommend interviewing this candidate for Auster at Dome Mountain?

**Yes — but conditionally, as a Director of Operations reading this cold.**

We're a new property. Every early hire sets the tone for how guests and the local community experience us, so I can't afford to gamble on someone who's all polish and no hands — but I also can't afford to pass on someone genuinely capable just because he described himself modestly.

What this page proves cleanly: he shows up, he works hard, and he's done real physical labor under real conditions. The bike-courier detail is the kind of specific, checkable claim I trust more than "hard worker" on a hundred other applications. He's also clearly thought this through — exact availability, relocation, openness on housing.

What it doesn't prove is the half of the job that worries me most: leading guests on a trail, keeping them safe around wildlife, making fly-fishing or a hike feel like an experience worth what they paid for. On that, this page tells me nothing — not that he can't do it, only that he hasn't told me whether he can. Given that gap sits directly on top of guest safety in bear and water country, I was never going to extend an offer off a bio page alone regardless. So the real question isn't "hire or not" — it's "worth thirty minutes or not."

It is. One conversation about whether he's ever led anyone outdoors, whether he's genuinely comfortable talking with strangers for hours at a time, and whether he's willing to get Wilderness First Aid certified before the season starts would tell me almost everything this page can't. If those answers are good, the design background and land training stop being things I have to explain away and become a real asset. If the answers are weak, I'd still consider him for a facilities/grounds-focused role, just not the full hybrid position as posted.

**The page earns him the conversation. The conversation has to earn him the job.**
